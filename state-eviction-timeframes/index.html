State Eviction Timeframes

How fast an eviction can lawfully move in every state and D.C.

The eviction clock runs ten times faster in some states than in others. Rebuilt stage-by-stage from each state's current statutes and court rules — the nonpayment eviction track, verified against primary law — the fastest lawful floor is 5 days; the slowest is 56. Most states set no upper limit at all.

Read this before using these numbers. These are statutory clocks, rebuilt element-by-element from each state's current eviction law and verified against primary sources in July 2026 — not how long a typical case takes. The floor is the fastest the law permits a nonpayment eviction to move when nothing slows it; real cases usually run longer. 34 of 51 jurisdictions set no statutory ceiling, so the slow end is unbounded by law. Stages marked ° have no statutory timing at all and count zero toward the floor. Day counts are taken at face value (business, judicial, and calendar days are not converted — a "7 business day" notice spans nine or more calendar days). Federally connected housing — public housing, Section 8, LIHTC, and homes with federally backed mortgages — gets 30 days' notice under the CARES Act regardless of state law. If you use this page for research, validate the current timelines and estimates against the cited statutes first — legislatures move, and several of these clocks changed while this page was being built. Figure I

An eviction runs four clocks in sequence.

Every eviction moves through the same four stages. The clock in this analysis starts when rent is missed and ends when the tenant must be out. Each stage adds days; the state's total is the sum.

GraceStage 1
Days the law inserts between rent falling due and the eviction clock starting. Most states: none — the notice can issue at once. Six jurisdictions verifiably delay it: Rhode Island (15), Connecticut (9), Maine (7), Oregon (7), South Dakota (3), North Dakota (3).
NoticeStage 2
The written warning — a Notice to Pay or Quit — and the days the tenant has to pay or leave before the landlord can file in court.
CourtStage 3
Filing the case, serving the summons, the tenant's response window, and the wait for a hearing and a judgment.
RemovalStage 4
After judgment: the court issues a writ, the sheriff serves it, and the tenant is given a final window to move before a forced lockout.

Methodology & sources

This page presents the Eviction Research Network's state time-to-evict dataset, rebuilt element-by-element from primary law — each state's current statutes and court rules — and verified in July 2026. It follows the nonpayment track, the modal eviction. It supersedes both the 2019 attorney-survey compilation first visualized by the Las Vegas Review-Journal and the commercial timeline tables that a stage-by-stage audit showed to be unreliable (see Verification below).

How the numbers are built

  • Each state's floor is the sum of the statutorily mandated minimum waits on the nonpayment track — grace (only where the law delays the clock), the nonpayment notice, court (filing to judgment), and removal (judgment to lockout) — every element traced to a pinpoint citation with its effective date.
  • A ceiling is shown only where the law caps every stage; 34 of 51 jurisdictions set no statutory ceiling, and their slow end is reported as open, not invented.
  • Stages where the law sets no timing at all (e.g., Indiana's hearing schedule, Nevada's summary-eviction hearing) contribute zero to the floor and are marked ° — those floors are understatements by construction.
  • Day counts are taken at face value as the law states them: business, judicial, and calendar days are not converted, so mixed-unit floors are conservative.
  • Worked example — D.C.: grace 0 (the 5-day rule is late-fee-only) · notice 10 (D.C. Law 26-80, eff. 12/31/2025) · court ≥14 (summons window; no statutory cap) · removal 23–77 (21-day eviction-date notice, since 2019) → floor 47, ceiling open.

Verification (July 2026)

Every stage of every state's row was checked against primary law — statutes, court rules, and judicial-branch publications — in a July 2026 verification pass (twelve research sweeps under evidence-only rules; pinpoint citations throughout). The verdict: grace and notice figures are mostly sound; the court stage is not. Thirty-four of 51 jurisdictions carry at least one stage that conflicts with primary law; the court stage is fully confirmed in only two (Michigan and Louisiana), and Louisiana is the only fully confirmed row. The compilation's recurring failures: converting statutory ceilings ("trial within 60 days of service") into additive waits, and counting no-cause termination notices as the nonpayment clock. Several rows were also outdated by recent law — D.C. (Dec. 2025), Virginia (July 2026), Hawaii (Feb. 2026), Minnesota (2024), Georgia (2024), South Dakota (2024), Oregon (2023), Rhode Island (2022). Following that audit, every figure on this page was rebuilt from the verified statutory chains; the original compilation is retained in the dataset as a comparison layer, and the full corrections report — citations and effective dates for every element — lives with the dataset (data/time_to_evict/verification/).

Federal overlay

State floors apply only to non-covered private housing. The CARES Act 30-day notice to vacate remains in effect (15 U.S.C. §9058(c) has no sunset; CRS, May 2026) for federally connected properties — public housing, Section 8, LIHTC, USDA programs, and homes with federally backed mortgages — and HUD's 30-day nonpayment-notice rule (effective Jan. 13, 2025) still binds public housing and project-based assistance, its attempted revocation indefinitely delayed after litigation. A pending bill, H.R. 1078, would repeal the CARES notice.
